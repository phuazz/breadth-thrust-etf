Deployable strategy

Top-K-by-breadth sector rotation

Each Friday close, compute % above 200d MA for each of the 11 ETFs. Rank them. Hold the top K = 7 next week, weighted by breadth (highest-breadth sector gets the largest slice). Refit K only annually on expanding-window Sharpe — the choice has been stable at K = 5 or 7 throughout the backtest.

  1. Every Friday close: pull the 11 ETF rosters, compute % of constituents above their own 200d simple MA.
  2. Rank ETFs by breadth. Take the top 7.
  3. Weight each by its breadth share: weight_i = breadth_i / Σ(top-7 breadths). High-breadth sectors get more capital, weak ones less.
  4. Rebalance to the new mix. The Trade Explorer tab shows the exact weights at every rebalance.
  5. Once a year, re-evaluate K ∈ {3, 5, 7}. So far the answer has been K = 7 for the last three years.

Strategy B — Phase 2

Asset-class momentum rotation

A separate strategy that operates at the asset-class level instead of within US sectors. 14 broad ETFs across US equity, international developed, emerging markets, real estate, commodities, and bonds. Each Friday close, rank by distance above own 200d MA. Hold the top K with positive signal, weight by signal strength; idle capital sits in IEF (intermediate Treasury) as a cash proxy. This is "which asset classes should I be in at all?" — orthogonal to Strategy A's "within US equities, which sector?".

  1. Each Friday close, compute (price − MA200) / MA200 for each of the 14 asset-class ETFs.
  2. Drop any ETF with negative signal (price below its 200d MA — in a downtrend).
  3. Rank survivors. Take the top K = 7.
  4. Weight by signal share. If only N < K ETFs have positive signal, allocate (N/K) of capital to them and the remaining (K − N)/K to IEF as cash proxy. Built-in cash floor when the world is broadly weak — unlike Strategy A which stays 100% invested.
  5. Rebalance to the new mix. Costs 5 bps × turnover.

Strategy C — Phase 3 (thematic sleeve)

Thematic momentum rotation

A third sleeve for catching secular trends that don't fit traditional sectors (AI, cybersecurity, clean energy, biotech, blockchain, etc). Same momentum signal as Strategy B applied to 16 thematic ETFs, with extra guardrails to limit fad-chasing. Sized at 10% of the combined portfolio — designed as optionality on the next AI-style bull run, not a primary alpha source.

  1. Each Friday close, compute distance above 200d MA for each of the 16 thematic ETFs.
  2. Hard signal floor: only consider ETFs with signal ≥ +5% above their 200d MA (not just positive). This filters out marginal "in an uptrend" cases that often reverse.
  3. Rank the survivors. Take the top K = 4.
  4. Per-ETF cap of 35% of Strategy C: even if one thematic runs hard alone (e.g. ARKK 2020-21), the strategy cannot concentrate beyond a third in any single ETF. Spilled weight redistributes pro-rata among the others.
  5. Cash floor: when fewer than K candidates clear the +5% threshold, the deficit sits in IEF (intermediate Treasury) as cash proxy.

Honest assessment — Strategy C does not improve risk-adjusted returns

The empirical record over 2018-2026 (7.5 years) tells a clear story: Strategy C standalone has lower Sharpe than A or B and the worst drawdown of the three (-43.7%). Walk-forward Sharpe is +0.36, roughly half of the in-sample +0.71 — exactly the fad-chasing degradation thematic ETFs are prone to. The 10% sleeve in the 45/45/10 blend costs 0.02 Sharpe (1.09 → 1.07) and adds ~1pp to max drawdown (-20.2% → -21.2%) in exchange for a modest +0.4pp CAGR pickup.

It is included as optionality: if the next AI / clean-energy / biotech bull is bigger than the ones the backtest contains, Strategy C is the sleeve that captures it. If the next decade looks like the last, Strategy C is a small drag. If your primary goal is risk-adjusted return, skip C and run the 50/50 A:B blend.

How to choose your blend — and what the data actually says

If you want best risk-adjusted return: 50/50 A:B (no C). It posts the highest Sharpe of any variant tested (~+1.09 on the common 2018-2026 window). Diversification benefit is real — A and B are negatively correlated on drawdowns: when A drops on a cyclical sector unwind, B rotates into bonds and cuts losses; when B is sideways, A's relative-strength tilt still earns a return.

If you want optionality on the next thematic bull run (AI, clean energy, biotech, etc): 45/45/10 A:B:C. Sharpe drops a touch (~+1.07) and max DD widens by ~1pp, but you capture ~+0.4pp of CAGR from the thematic sleeve. The 10% size cap means even if C blows up 50% in a year, the combined portfolio bleeds only ~5% from that sleeve — a tolerable insurance premium.

If you want maximum CAGR and tolerate big drawdowns: Strategy A alone (~+17.5% CAGR but -30.7% max DD). The blends trade ~3pp of CAGR for ~10pp of drawdown reduction — usually a good trade for most investors.

If drawdown control is paramount: 30/70 A:B. Sharpe still strong (~+1.08), CAGR ~+13%, max DD only ~-17%.

The meta-rotation variant (own only A or only B each week, picking by trailing 6-month Sharpe) performs worse than either strategy alone. The lookback lags too much; by the time it identifies that B is winning, A has often started winning again. Static blends beat dynamic blends in this dataset.

Caveat on Strategy C: the thematic sleeve has walk-forward Sharpe +0.36 (vs in-sample +0.71) — significant degradation, the classic fad-chasing pattern. The 5% signal floor and 35% per-ETF cap are guardrails, but C is genuinely riskier than A or B. Use it as 10% optionality, not as a core sleeve.

Test 1 — Walk-forward L selection (the headline test)

Annual refit: train on expanding window ending each Dec 31, apply the fitted L to the next calendar year. Concatenate the test segments. This is the realistic OOS Sharpe with no in-sample threshold selection.

Test 2 — Borrow cost on 50/150 leverage

The 50/150 variant assumes free margin. Recomputed Sharpe with 5%/yr broker call rate on the 50% extra notional during the long-state. Shows the practical drag.

Test 3 — Sub-period Sharpe (strategy vs buy-and-hold)

Per-regime Sharpe decomposition. Identifies whether the in-sample edge is regime-driven (e.g., concentrated in one favourable period) or genuine across regimes. Δ = strategy Sharpe − BH Sharpe (positive = strategy beats BH).

Test 4 — Bootstrap Sharpe confidence interval

Block bootstrap (60-day blocks, 1,000 samples) of the daily strategy return series. The point Sharpe is the in-sample number; the bootstrap p5/p50/p95 give the realistic range when paths could have differed.

Test 5 — MA-period robustness

Was MA200 specifically the "right" lookback, or just a lucky single point? Sweep MA period ∈ {100, 150, 200, 250, 300} for two key ETFs and re-find the best L for each. If MA200 is an obvious peak, the choice was in-sample-tuned and adds another layer of bias.

Test 6 — Rebalance frequency: daily vs weekly vs monthly

For each ETF (using its in-sample-best L), apply the strategy at daily / weekly Friday / weekly Monday / bi-weekly Friday / month-end rebalances. Compare Sharpe, max DD, and annual turnover (cost driver). Cheaper turnover at similar Sharpe = better deployment choice.

Test 7 — Walk-forward L × rebalance cadence (the key cross-test)

Combine the two robustness tests: walk-forward L refit AND vary rebalance cadence. For each (ETF × cadence) cell, refit L annually on expanding train and apply that L to the test segment using the specified cadence. The values are the realistic OOS Sharpe.

Test 8 — Fixed L = 60 across ALL ETFs (NO tuning whatsoever)

The most overfit-resistant test possible: apply a single fixed L threshold uniformly across all 11 ETFs. No per-ETF tuning, no refit, no optimisation. The "what does an investor with zero tuning skill but knowing the basic Zweig rule earn?" baseline. Compare to the in-sample-tuned L and the walk-forward refit L on the same ETF.

Test 9 — Extended history 2000-2026 (incl. dot-com bust + 2008 GFC)

The strategy explained, plain English

Pick the strongest seven sectors of the US stock market each week. Own them. Update weekly. That is the whole strategy.

What is a "sector"? The US stock market is split into industry groups — Financials, Energy, Health Care, Industrials, Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Utilities, Materials, Communication Services, Real Estate — plus a few broad-market and concentrated picks (S&P 500, NASDAQ-100, Semiconductors, Small-cap 600). We use 14 ETFs that represent these groups. (NASDAQ-100 already gives us the large-cap tech exposure; we pruned the S&P 500 Info Tech ETF in May 2026 because it duplicated the NASDAQ-100 too closely.)

How do we judge which sector is "strong"? Every sector contains 30 to 500 individual companies. We count what percentage of those companies are currently trading above their own 200-day moving average. A stock above its 200-day average is in an uptrend; below it, a downtrend. So if 85% of the companies in a sector are in uptrends, that sector is strong — broadly strong, not just lifted by a few mega-caps. We call this number the breadth of the sector. It ranges from 0% (everything broken) to 100% (everything in an uptrend).

1
Every Friday after market close, look up the constituents of each of the 11 ETFs and compute the breadth (% above 200d MA) for each one.
2
Rank the 11 ETFs by breadth. Whichever seven are highest, those are your portfolio next week.
3
Weight them by breadth share. If the top seven breadths are (85, 80, 70, 65, 55, 50, 45) — sum is 450 — then their portfolio weights are 85/450 = 19%, 80/450 = 18%, and so on. The strongest sector gets more of your money than the weakest of the seven.
4
Re-check next Friday. Some sectors will have weakened and dropped out of the top 7; others will have strengthened and entered. Sell what dropped out, buy what came in, adjust the rest. Costs are about 5 basis points (0.05%) per unit of weight change — a typical retail ETF brokerage fee. The Trade Explorer tab shows every single rebalance.

What this strategy is NOT doing. It is not trying to time the market — there is no "stay in cash if everything looks bad" rule. It is always 100% invested. It is also not trying to predict which individual sector will go up next. It is doing something simpler: own whatever is leading, trim whatever is lagging. Markets reward this pattern more often than they punish it, because trends in market breadth tend to persist for weeks-to-months at a time.

Why we trust it. The "Robustness" tab shows the strategy holds up when we apply the standard quant-research stress tests: walk-forward validation (refitting the only free parameter, K = the number of sectors to own, each year on out-of-sample data), bootstrap confidence intervals, sub-period decomposition through bear markets, sensitivity to the moving-average lookback and the rebalance frequency. The walk-forward Sharpe ratio is approximately 1.05 with no degradation from the in-sample number — that is unusual and is the main reason we picked this paradigm over per-ETF threshold tuning or fixed-threshold timing.

Caveats and what would kill it: a regime where all 11 sectors fall together (2008-style systemic crisis) hurts the strategy because owning the "strongest" of a falling universe is still owning falling stuff. We do not currently hedge or shift to cash. A modest improvement on the to-do list is to add a "if median breadth across all sectors is below 40%, reduce gross exposure to 50%" overlay — but we have not yet validated it OOS so it is not in the headline numbers.

Signal definition (formal)

Breadth indicator (Strategy A): % of an ETF's point-in-time constituents whose closing price is above their own 200-day simple moving average. Computed daily; constituents reset weekly from the iShares UK holdings endpoint.

Momentum signal (Strategy B): distance above own 200-day MA per ETF, (close − MA200) / MA200. Positive = uptrend, negative = downtrend. Computed daily from yfinance adjusted close.

The constituent set is broader than the S&P 500 Real Estate sub-sector, but the breadth metric (% above 200d MA) is still constituent-relative and remains comparable.

Data sources: iShares UK holdings endpoint for point-in-time constituents (weekly Friday snapshots back to 2018-01-05), yfinance for adjusted close prices of constituents and trading proxies. All data is free and reproducible from the scripts in this repo.

OOS validation

Both Strategy A and Strategy B are validated walk-forward (see Robustness → Test 10 for Strategy A's annual K refit and Strategy B's tab for its own walk-forward). The breadth signal itself was earlier validated via a 2022-09-08 train/test split on a composite breadth indicator: the train-half winner held up on the test half (train Sharpe +0.94 → test Sharpe +1.42 on SOXX; train winner ranked #1 of 11 cells on test grid). The current top-K rotation paradigm strips away the per-ETF threshold tuning that earlier work relied on, eliminating most of the in-sample selection bias.

Best practices for parameter optimisation (lessons from this project)

The Robustness tab demonstrates that the headline Sharpe was materially inflated by in-sample optimisation. Nine rules of thumb learned the hard way:

  • Always report walk-forward (or train/test) Sharpe alongside in-sample. The in-sample number is biased upward by ~0.3 Sharpe in our case. If you only have IS, halve your conviction.
  • Robust over optimal. A parameter that's "second-best" but stable across sub-windows is more deployable than a parameter that's "best" but jumps each refit. Our walk-forward L sequence for CSP1 was [55, 80, 75, 75, 75] — wildly unstable; deploying any single one of those would have given different results.
  • Reduce degrees of freedom. Per-ETF tuning adds N free parameters. A single global parameter (e.g., L=60 for all 11 ETFs) had ZERO tuning cost, and beat walk-forward L on 8 of 11 ETFs. Less is more.
  • Use external priors. If literature gives you a number (Zweig's L=60 breadth threshold), USE IT. A prior-based value has no overfit cost; a data-driven value does.
  • Lower the rebalance frequency to lower in-sample noise transmission. Daily rebalancing transmits each fitted-L noise into more daily decisions. Bi-weekly rebalancing reduces the effective number of "actions" per unit time, smoothing the noise.
  • Stress-test by regime, not just full-window Sharpe. The sub-period decomposition showed our strategy underperformed BH in 2019 pre-COVID, 2022 inflation shock, and 2023 AI rally. Full-window Sharpe hid this. Decompose by regime — if performance is concentrated in one period, you're sampling-lucky.
  • Avoid the joint maximum across multiple parameters. If you can choose L, MA-period, cadence, AND base/thrust allocation, picking the joint argmax has compounded selection bias. Pick one parameter at a time on independent data, or use a fixed-parameter version.
  • If walk-forward Sharpe ≤ BH Sharpe, abandon the strategy. No amount of in-sample tweaking saves a strategy whose held-out performance is no better than the passive benchmark. CSP1 fails this test (WF 0.57 vs BH 0.84); SOXX with bi-weekly cadence passes (1.05 vs 0.98).
  • Prefer relative signals to absolute thresholds. "Top K by breadth" is a relative statement that self-normalises across regimes; "breadth ≥ L" is an absolute threshold that has to be re-tuned every regime. Test 10 shows the cross-sectional rotation paradigm has zero walk-forward degradation, while threshold-based timing degrades ~0.3 Sharpe. Whenever a signal can be expressed as a cross-sectional rank instead of a level, prefer the rank version.

Practical deployment translation. The strategy that survives all the tests is paradigm 3 from Test 10: own the top K = 5-7 ETFs by current MA200 breadth, weight by breadth excess over the cross-sectional median, rebalance weekly Friday, no per-ETF threshold tuning, refit K only annually. Walk-forward Sharpe ≈ 1.05 with no degradation from in-sample. The previous "fixed L=60 on IUIT / CNDX / IUES" recommendation was correct given the framing of paradigm 2, but paradigm 3 is strictly better — it owns the same sectors when they lead, drops them when they lag, and never bets on a single ETF being "above the L-line" in isolation. The original headline Sharpe 1.04 for CSP1 single-ETF was an artifact; the headline Sharpe 1.05 for top-K rotation is honest.

Universe expansion — does adding more ETFs help?

The universe went from 11 to 15 ETFs in May 2026 by adding the 3 missing S&P 500 sectors (Materials IUMS, Communication Services IUCM, plus IUSP as a Real Estate proxy) and IDP6 (S&P SmallCap 600). The headline top-K rotation Sharpe moved from +0.99 (11 ETFs, K=7 Weekly Fri) to +0.95 (15 ETFs, same K and cadence) — a small decrease. Annual turnover rose from ~10× to ~14× because more ETFs cluster near the K-th breadth boundary and shuffle in and out more often.

Why expanding within the same rotation didn't help: the strategy ranks ETFs by breadth and takes the top K, so adding ETFs at the same K dilutes conviction. The strongest sectors are still strongest, but extra candidates compete for the marginal slot. Cost goes up; signal goes down slightly. The strategy was already universe-saturated around 11 ETFs for K=7. The lesson: adding ETFs of the same flavour to an existing top-K rotation produces diminishing or negative returns. The dimension that actually adds information is orthogonality.

Universe pruning (May 2026, post-expansion): the correlation diagnostic (Robustness Test 12) flagged IUIT (S&P 500 Info Tech) at 0.97 breadth correlation with CNDX (NASDAQ-100). Since CNDX is the more-traded variant of the same large-cap tech exposure (via QQQ which has ~10× IUIT's liquidity), keeping IUIT was double-counting. Pruning it brought the active universe to 14 ETFs. Result: walk-forward Sharpe improved +0.034 (from +0.955 to +0.989), turnover dropped 5%, and every multi-strategy blend variant gained +0.009 to +0.014 Sharpe. Pruning a redundant ETF helped slightly more than carrying it.

That motivated Phase 2: build a separate Strategy B at the asset-class level (see the Strategy B and Multi-Strategy A+B tabs). Strategy B operates on 14 broad asset-class ETFs (US equity / international / EM / real estate / commodities / bonds), ranking by distance above the ETF's own 200d MA — at the index level rather than the constituent level. Empirical result: combining A and B 50/50 gives Sharpe +1.10 with max DD -21% — better than either strategy alone (A: +0.95, DD -31%; B: +0.78, DD -14%). The 18-year Strategy B backtest (2008-2026, including GFC and COVID) shows the regime-following behaviour: it rotates into bonds and gold in crisis periods and into equities in expansions. Diversification benefit is real — the combined Sharpe exceeds the weighted sum of individual Sharpes because A and B are negatively correlated on drawdowns.

The meta-rotation variant (own only A or only B each week, switching based on trailing 6-month Sharpe) destroys value — the lookback lags too much and the meta-signal chases the previous regime. Static blends beat dynamic blends.

Caveats

What this study does not capture:

  • Slippage on rebalances: rebalances assume mid-spread fills. Worst-case slippage may add another 5-10 bps per rebalance on top of the 5 bps transaction cost already modelled.
  • Survivorship in some price series: a few historically-acquired constituents (~5-15% of the early-window roster for some ETFs) are missing from yfinance, biasing early-window breadth measurements toward survivors. Less of an issue post-2020.
  • Strategy A common window 2019-2026 was favourable for long-only equity: US large-cap returned ~17% annualised. Strategy B's 2008-2026 window covers two real crises (GFC, COVID, 2022 rates shock) which is part of why its max DD is much lower. The 50/50 blend inherits some of that — but a sustained bear market would still stress the combined approach.
  • Concentration risk in Strategy A: even at K = 7 the top breadth tilts toward 1-2 dominant sectors, so a semis or energy reversal can move 10-20% of NAV in a week. The 50/50 blend with Strategy B cushions but does not eliminate this.
  • Strategy B cash-floor mechanic: when fewer than K asset classes are positive-trend, the deficit sits in IEF (intermediate Treasury). Earns ~3-4% Treasury carry but introduces small duration risk in a rising-rate shock.
